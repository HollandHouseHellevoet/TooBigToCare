## Part II. The Game

### Ch 4. The Price Isn't the Price. LONG.

**Proves:** Medicare is twelve parallel payment systems, and the one that applies is selected by classification, not by the care delivered.

**Sourcing.** Primary in-text source for each system is the MedPAC Payment Basics one-pager series. Regulatory anchor (42 CFR and SSA provisions) in the source brackets for verification. Annual Federal Register rule citations held for endnotes, not in the body.

1. The Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (MPFS) pays physicians and certain non-physician clinicians on a per-service basis, calculated as the sum of work, practice expense, and malpractice Relative Value Units multiplied by an annually set conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Physician and Other Health Professional Services; 42 CFR 414 subpart B]
2. The Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) pays hospital outpatient departments on a per-encounter basis using Ambulatory Payment Classifications, each with a scaled relative weight multiplied by the OPPS conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Outpatient Hospital Services; 42 CFR 419]
3. The Inpatient Prospective Payment System (IPPS) pays acute-care hospitals a single bundled payment per discharge based on Medicare Severity Diagnosis-Related Groups, with adjustments for wage index, disproportionate share, and graduate medical education. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Hospital Acute Inpatient Services; 42 CFR 412]
4. The Ambulatory Surgical Center Payment System pays Medicare-certified ambulatory surgical centers on a per-procedure basis using rates set as a fraction of the OPPS APC for the same procedure, updated annually by the ASC conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Ambulatory Surgical Center Services; 42 CFR 416]
5. The Skilled Nursing Facility Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified skilled nursing facilities a per-diem rate adjusted for patient case mix under the Patient Driven Payment Model, covering nursing, therapy, and non-therapy ancillary components. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Skilled Nursing Facility Services; 42 CFR 413 subpart J]
6. The Home Health Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified home health agencies a case-mix adjusted payment per 30-day period of care under the Patient-Driven Groupings Model. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Home Health Care Services; 42 CFR 484 subpart E]
7. The Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility Prospective Payment System pays inpatient rehabilitation facilities and distinct-part rehabilitation units a per-discharge payment based on Case Mix Groups, with the facility required to meet the 60 percent rule on qualifying patient conditions to retain IRF classification. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart P]
8. The Long-Term Care Hospital Prospective Payment System pays long-term care hospitals a per-discharge rate using Medicare Severity Long-Term Care DRGs, with a statutorily-defined site-neutral rate applying to cases that do not meet patient-criteria thresholds. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Long-Term Care Hospital Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart O]
9. The Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System pays freestanding psychiatric hospitals and distinct-part psychiatric units a per-diem rate adjusted for patient characteristics, length of stay, and facility features. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Psychiatric Hospital Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart N]
10. The End-Stage Renal Disease Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified dialysis facilities a single bundled per-treatment rate covering dialysis services, most injectable drugs, laboratory services, and supplies. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Outpatient Dialysis Services; 42 CFR 413 subpart H]
11. Medicare pays hospice care to Medicare-certified hospices on a per-diem basis at four distinct levels of care (routine home care, continuous home care, inpatient respite care, and general inpatient care), subject to an aggregate annual cap per beneficiary. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Hospice Services; 42 CFR 418 subpart G]
12. Critical Access Hospitals are paid 101 percent of reasonable cost for Medicare inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, and laboratory services, bypassing the prospective payment systems entirely, under designation requirements that include rural location and a 25-bed limit. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Critical Access Hospital Services; 42 CFR 413.70 and 42 CFR 485 subpart F]
13. Each of the twelve payment systems enumerated above is governed by its own statutory authority, its own annual rulemaking cycle, its own conversion factor or update mechanism, and its own set of eligibility rules for which entities can bill under it. [Source: synthesis of the twelve statutory and regulatory citations above]
14. The payment system that applies to a given Medicare service is selected by the CMS enrollment classification of the billing entity at the time of service, not by the clinical content of the service performed. [Source: 42 CFR 424 subpart P, CMS enrollment and billing entity classification rules]

### Ch 10. The Moat. MEDIUM.

**Proves:** the eight streams are not independent, each one unlocks or protects the next, forming a moat rather than a list.

1. [Source: 42 USC 1396r-4(d) DSH qualification; 42 USC 256b(a)(4)(L) 340B DSH pathway; *The Systematic Advantage*, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, Medicaid supplemental and 340B line items]
2. The nonprofit or public ownership status that most 340B-eligible hospitals satisfy to access 340B via the DSH pathway under 42 USC 256b(a)(4)(L) overlaps with the 26 USC 501(c)(3) designation that simultaneously unlocks the $37.4 billion federal, state, and local tax exemption stream, such that the same corporate structure positions a hospital for two streams at once and excludes for-profit competitors from each. [Source: 42 USC 256b(a)(4) eligibility requirements; 26 USC 501(c)(3); *The Systematic Advantage*, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, nonprofit tax exemption and 340B line items]
3. Graduate Medical Education payments under 42 USC 1395ww(h) and 42 USC 1395ww(d)(5)(B) require Medicare certification as a teaching hospital operating an approved graduate medical residency program, which is an institutional, capital, and infrastructure threshold that independent physician practices and small hospitals cannot satisfy, binding the $16.2 billion GME stream to the same scale prerequisite that gates the other streams. [Source: 42 USC 1395ww(h); 42 CFR 413.75 teaching hospital requirements; *The Systematic Advantage*, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, GME line item]
4. Hospital-based billing classification under 42 CFR 413.65 converts acquired physician practice volume into hospital outpatient department volume, which flows into the hospital's case mix used in the IPPS Disproportionate Share Hospital adjustment under 42 CFR 412.106 and the Indirect Medical Education adjustment under 42 CFR 412.105, such that each site-of-service conversion increases the hospital's exposure to the Medicaid supplemental, GME, and 340B streams in subsequent program years. 5. Medicare Advantage overpayment capture under 42 USC 1395w-23 requires the hospital system to own or contract with a Medicare Advantage plan and to operate the risk-adjustment coding infrastructure that drives the overpayment, a combination of capital, scale, and vertically integrated delivery that independent physician practices cannot assemble, tying the $17.5 billion MA overpayment stream to the same institutional scale required for the other streams. [Source: 42 USC 1395w-23 capitation methodology; MedPAC annual Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment chapters; *The Systematic Advantage*, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, MA overpayments line item]
6. Rural hospital designations under 42 CFR 485 subpart F (Critical Access Hospital), the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021 (Rural Emergency Hospital), and Medicare rural classifications (Sole Community Hospital, Rural Referral Center) unlock participation in Rural Health Transformation funding, serve as an eligibility pathway for 340B covered entity status under 42 USC 256b(a)(4), and qualify for a subset of HRSA rural grant programs, stacking three of the eight streams on a single facility classification. Surplus generated by any one of the eight streams can be reinvested by a hospital system in physician practice acquisitions, which expand the hospital's billing footprint and increase its exposure to Medicaid supplemental payments, 340B purchasing volume, GME resident counts, and site-of-service classification in subsequent program years, producing a reinforcing cycle in which each stream enables the expansion of the others. [Source: synthesis of claims 1 through 6; PAI/Avalere 2024 acquisition volume data referenced in Ch 7 claim 1; Avalere September 2024 pre-post transition analysis referenced in Ch 7 claim 5]
8. Independent physician practices are excluded from all eight streams simultaneously because each stream is conditioned on an institutional classification, facility designation, nonprofit or public corporate structure, or scale requirement that independent practice does not satisfy as a matter of program design, making exclusion from one stream structurally synonymous with exclusion from all eight. [Source: eligibility rules cited in Ch 9 claims 2 through 9; eight-stream program architecture from *The Systematic Advantage*, Dutch Rojas, January 2026]

## Part IV. The Rules

### Ch 12. The Origin. MEDIUM.

**Proves:** the payment architecture was not discovered, it was written into the 1997 Balanced Budget Act and has compounded every year since.

**Ordering note.** Part IV is ordered by argumentative function, not by date. The chronological order of the statutes named in Part IV is Stark Law (1989 and 1993, treated in Ch 15), the BBA 1997 (treated in this chapter), ACA Section 6001 (2010, treated in Ch 14), and the No Surprises Act (2020, treated in Ch 16), with state Certificate of Need laws (Ch 13) operating across states from the 1960s forward. Part IV walks the statutes in the order they do thesis work, not the order in which Congress passed them.

1. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 (Public Law 105-33), enacted August 5, 1997, directed the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services to establish the Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) for hospital outpatient services under 42 USC 1395l(t), creating the first prospective federal payment rate structure distinct from the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule. [Source: P.L. 105-33 Title IV Subtitle C; 42 USC 1395l(t)]
2. The Medicare payment provisions of the BBA 1997 were drafted and marked up within the House Ways and Means Committee and the Senate Finance Committee, with the revenue-affecting Medicare Part A and Part B provisions originating from Ways and Means as the constitutionally required House origination for revenue bills under Article I Section 7 of the U.S. Constitution. [Source: House Ways and Means Committee records for the 105th Congress; U.S. Constitution Article I Section 7; waysandmeans.rojasreport.com for current committee jurisdiction over Medicare payment legislation]
3. Implementation of the Outpatient Prospective Payment System under the BBA 1997 mandate took effect through the CMS final rule published at 65 FR 18434 on April 7, 2000, which established the first Ambulatory Payment Classification groups, the first OPPS conversion factor, and the distinction between hospital-based and office-based payment rates for equivalent clinical services. [Source: 65 FR 18434, April 7, 2000; CMS OPPS implementation history]
4. Subsequent federal legislation repeatedly expanded the BBA 1997 architecture rather than narrowed it, including the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 (P.L. 108-173) which created the Medicare Advantage and Part D programs and expanded 340B eligibility, the Affordable Care Act of 2010 (P.L. 111-148) which added Section 6001's construction freeze on physician-owned hospitals and expanded Medicaid Disproportionate Share Hospital qualification thresholds, and the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2015 (P.L. 114-74) which enacted Section 603's off-campus hospital outpatient department site-neutral carve-out. [Source: P.L. 108-173; P.L. 111-148; P.L. 114-74]
5. Each successive OPPS final rule from CY2001 through CY2026 has preserved the core structural element of the BBA 1997 architecture (a distinct prospective payment schedule for hospital-based services billed at higher rates than the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule for equivalent services) while adjusting individual APC payment weights and the annual OPPS conversion factor through the yearly rulemaking cycle. [Source: annual CMS OPPS final rules from CY2001 (65 FR 18434) through the CY2026 final rule (CMS-1834-FC)]
6. The BBA 1997 also established the Sustainable Growth Rate formula governing Medicare physician payment updates under 42 USC 1395w-4(f), which capped Medicare Physician Fee Schedule growth below the rate of inflation for most years of its operation and was repealed and replaced by the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act of 2015 (Public Law 114-10). [Source: P.L. 105-33; 42 USC 1395w-4(f); P.L. 114-10]
7. The Medicare Physician Fee Schedule conversion factor was 7.6 percent lower in real terms in 2023 than in 1998, while the OPPS conversion factor grew over the same period by statutory market-basket updates, producing a widening of the site-of-service payment gap beyond anything in the 1997 statutory design. [Source: HAP Radiology historical MPFS conversion factor analysis; CMS OPPS annual final rules CY2001 through CY2026]
8. The BBA 1997 is the single legislative event most directly responsible for creating the distinct OPPS payment schedule that underlies the site-of-service stream in the $274.2 billion architecture enumerated in Ch 9, and subsequent federal legislation has amended the BBA 1997 framework without replacing its core structural choice of prospective payment systems billed at different rates by different institutional classifications for the same clinical services. [Source: statutory history of P.L. 105-33 and its amendments through 2025; Ch 9 claim 7 on site-of-service line item]

### Ch 13. The Gatekeeper. SHORT.

**Proves:** Certificate of Need laws let the incumbents decide who is allowed to compete with them.

1. Certificate of Need laws are state-level regulatory programs requiring prior government approval for new hospital construction, hospital bed expansion, major medical equipment purchases (including MRI, CT, linear accelerators, and other high-capital technologies), and in many states new ambulatory surgical centers or freestanding diagnostic facilities. [Source: National Conference of State Legislatures Certificate of Need tracker; state CON statute enumerations]
2. 34 states and the District of Columbia maintain active Certificate of Need programs covering at least one category of healthcare facility, with the scope of covered facility types varying substantially by state. [Source: Rojas Report Certificate of Need tracker, https://rojasreport.com/certificate-of-need]
3. Incumbent hospitals operating in a proposed new entrant's service area are granted formal standing under state CON statutes to file objections, submit competing impact analyses, and challenge CON applications in administrative hearings that determine whether a competitor receives permission to enter the market. [Source: state CON statutes, opposition standing provisions; state administrative law hearing records]
4. The Federal Trade Commission and Department of Justice jointly recommended repeal of Certificate of Need laws in a published 2008 joint statement on competition in healthcare, citing findings that CON programs impede new market entry, raise prices, and reduce access without delivering the community benefit their defenders claim, and the FTC has reiterated this position in subsequent reports and congressional testimony. [Source: FTC and DOJ joint statement on Certificate of Need laws, 2008; FTC Bureau of Economics reports on CON and subsequent commentary]
5. Empirical analyses of states that have repealed or substantially modified Certificate of Need programs find that repeal is not associated with the rural hospital closures, access losses, or quality declines that proponents of retention predicted, supporting the conclusion that CON's operational function is incumbent protection rather than access protection. [Source: Mercatus Center at George Mason University Certificate of Need research series; peer-reviewed studies on state CON repeal outcomes]

### Ch 14. The Freeze. LONG.

**Proves:** Section 6001 froze physician-owned hospitals at their 2010 footprint, eliminated 85, converted 76 more into joint ventures with hospital systems, and left 104 surviving as independent physician-owned facilities, removing the most efficient structural competitor in American medicine by statute.

1. Section 6001 of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (Public Law 111-148), enacted March 23, 2010, amended 42 USC 1395nn(i) to prohibit any new physician ownership of Medicare-participating hospitals and to freeze the existing physician-owned hospital footprint at its 2010 configuration. [Source: P.L. 111-148 Section 6001; 42 USC 1395nn(i)]
2. Under the grandfathering provisions of Section 6001, physician-owned hospitals operating as of March 23, 2010 were permitted to continue Medicare participation only under strict conditions, including a prohibition on increasing the number of licensed beds or operating rooms beyond the 2010 count, a prohibition on increasing the percentage of physician ownership above the 2010 level, and new disclosure obligations to patients. [Source: 42 USC 1395nn(i)(1); CMS implementing regulations at 42 CFR 411.362]
3. At the date of Section 6001 enactment, 265 physician-owned hospitals were operating as Medicare-participating hospitals in the United States, distributed across specialty and general acute care facilities in more than 30 states. [Source: poh.rojasreport.com, pre-ban POH roster as of March 2010]
4. As of current tracking, 104 of the original 265 physician-owned hospitals continue to operate as independent physician-owned hospitals under grandfathered status. [Source: poh.rojasreport.com, current POH tracker]
5. 85 of the original 265 physician-owned hospitals have been eliminated from the physician-owned hospital category by closure, loss of Medicare participation, sale to non-physician ownership without a joint venture structure, or conversion to non-hospital facility types. [Source: poh.rojasreport.com, eliminated POH tracker]
6. 76 of the original 265 physician-owned hospitals have been absorbed into joint venture arrangements with hospital systems, in which the hospital system co-owns the facility alongside the physician group under a modified ownership structure, converting facilities that competed against hospital systems before Section 6001 into partners of those same systems after Section 6001. [Source: poh.rojasreport.com, joint venture tracker]
7. 161 of the 265 physician-owned hospitals that existed at the date of Section 6001 enactment no longer operate as independent physician-owned competitors in the US hospital market (85 fully eliminated and 76 absorbed into joint ventures with hospital systems), leaving approximately 61 percent of the pre-ban physician-owned hospital universe outside the independent-competitor category. [Source: derivation from claims 3 through 6; poh.rojasreport.com]
8. Prior to the enactment of Section 6001 in 2010, the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice had examined physician-owned specialty hospitals in joint health care competition hearings and reports, finding no empirical evidence that physician-owned hospitals harmed patient care, raised prices, or reduced competition relative to non-physician-owned comparators. [Source: FTC and DOJ joint hearings on health care competition and law, 2003 through 2004; FTC and DOJ joint report "Improving Health Care: A Dose of Competition," 2004; subsequent FTC commentary on specialty hospitals through 2008]
9. Pre-ban cost and quality comparisons of physician-owned hospitals against matched non-physician-owned hospitals in CMS Hospital Compare data and in published peer-reviewed studies from 2005 through 2010 consistently found that physician-owned hospitals operated at comparable or lower cost per patient and achieved comparable or better clinical outcomes on standard CMS quality measures. [Source: CMS Hospital Compare archive data, pre-2010; peer-reviewed comparative studies in Health Affairs, Medical Care Research and Review, and Journal of the American College of Surgeons]
10. Chip Kahn, President and CEO of the Federation of American Hospitals from 2001 to 2025, stated in a June 2021 Advisory Board interview that "the current ban on physician-owned hospitals wouldn't be there if it wasn't for the Federation" and that he personally emailed talking points to Senate staffers during the ACA drafting process, confirming that Section 6001 was not an independent congressional judgment but an industry-authored provision inserted during legislative negotiations in which hospital industry support had become a condition of ACA passage. [Source: Advisory Board interview, Chip Kahn, June 2021, as documented at fah.rojasreport.com/pillars/poh-ban.html]
11. Section 6001's physician-owned hospital construction freeze was inserted into the Affordable Care Act during the Senate-side markup process rather than originating from the initial House-passed health reform legislation, without independent committee hearings specifically dedicated to the effect of the freeze on physician-owned hospital markets or a public cost-benefit analysis of the provision. [Source: Senate Finance Committee markup records for the 111th Congress; ACA legislative history; comparison of House H.R. 3962 and Senate H.R. 3590 as passed]
12. The net effect of Section 6001 was to remove the single most competitive structural alternative to the dominant hospital system model from the U.S. health care market by statute rather than by market outcome, converting 85 of 265 physician-owned hospitals into closures, 76 more into joint ventures with the very hospital systems they had competed against, and leaving 104 as grandfathered facilities frozen at their 2010 capacity and barred from expansion. [Source: synthesis of claims 1 through 11; poh.rojasreport.com aggregate finding]

### Ch 15. The Constraint. SHORT.

**Proves:** Stark Law restricts physician referral behavior while exempting hospital employment models, making scale the escape hatch from the referral rules.

1. The physician self-referral law, commonly called "Stark Law" after its primary sponsor Representative Pete Stark, is codified at 42 USC 1395nn and prohibits physicians from referring Medicare patients to designated health services entities in which the physician or an immediate family member has a financial relationship, unless a statutory or regulatory exception applies. [Source: 42 USC 1395nn; CMS Stark Law overview materials]
2. [Source: P.L. 101-239 Section 6204; P.L. 103-66 Section 13562; 42 USC 1395nn(h)(6) designated health services definition]
3. [Source: 42 USC 1395nn(e)(2); 42 CFR 411.357(c)]
4. The combined operation of the Stark prohibition on physician ownership of designated health services entities and the bona fide employment exception creates a structural asymmetry: an independent physician who co-owns an imaging center, physical therapy practice, or other designated-health-services facility cannot refer Medicare patients to that facility under Stark, but a physician employed by a hospital system can refer Medicare patients to the hospital's imaging centers, physical therapy practices, and other designated health services without any Stark violation. [Source: 42 USC 1395nn(a)(1) prohibition and 42 USC 1395nn(e)(2) employment exception read together; CMS Stark Law advisory opinions]
5. The practical effect of this asymmetry is that the lowest-friction path for a physician seeking to operate within integrated clinical delivery is employment by a hospital system rather than independent ownership of a practice with ancillary services, making scale (accessed via hospital employment) the operational escape hatch from the Stark referral constraint that independent physicians cannot use on their own. [Source: synthesis of claims 3 and 4; Stark Law regulatory and advisory opinion history]
