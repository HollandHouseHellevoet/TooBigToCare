Ch 1. The Same Procedure. SHORT.

Proves: the same procedure, performed by the same physician on the same patient, pays at different rates depending only on the building it happens in.

  1. [Source: Dutch Rojas operator record, Charlotte market, locality-adjusted Medicare ASC reimbursement. Precision note: the CY2026 national unadjusted ASC rate for CPT 47562 is $3,030.97 per CMS Addendum AA, cited in Ch 3]
  2. A physician-owned surgical facility in the Charlotte, North Carolina market is paid approximately $6,800 for the same laparoscopic cholecystectomy. [Source: Dutch Rojas operator record, Charlotte market, physician-owned surgical facility]
  3. Atrium Health's negotiated commercial rate for the same laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed at a hospital outpatient department in the Charlotte, North Carolina market is approximately $34,700. [Source: Dutch Rojas operator record, Atrium Health commercial contract, Charlotte market]
  4. The ratio between the highest facility payment in the three-tier comparison ($34,700 at Atrium Health) and the lowest ($4,200 at the ambulatory surgical center) for the identical surgical procedure is approximately 8.3 to 1. [Source: derived from claims 1 and 3]
  5. The clinical content across all three sites is the same surgical procedure performed by the same physician in approximately the same 45-minute operative window. [Source: Dutch Rojas operator record, clinical equivalence assertion]

Ch 2. The First Explanation That Isn't True. SHORT.

Proves: the standard justifications for the payment gap (sicker patients, higher acuity, EMTALA obligations) do not survive contact with the data.

  1. The Charlotte cholecystectomy case from Ch 1 establishes that the 8.3 to 1 facility fee gap cannot be explained by acuity differences, because the patient, the physician, and the 45-minute procedure were identical across all three sites. [Source: Dutch Rojas operator record, Ch 1 case]
  2. MedPAC's acuity-adjusted analyses of site-of-service cost differences between hospital outpatient departments and ambulatory surgical centers find that the payment gap persists after adjusting for patient case mix. [Source: MedPAC March report, site-of-service chapters]
  3. CBO site-neutral payment scoring has projected tens of billions of dollars in Medicare savings from eliminating the HOPD and ASC differential for equivalent services, without projecting access or acuity losses. [Source: CBO site-neutral savings scores, recent budget options reports]
  4. EMTALA (42 USC 1395dd) imposes screening and stabilization obligations on hospital emergency departments and does not apply to scheduled outpatient surgical services. [Source: 42 USC 1395dd]
  5. Uncompensated care expense as a share of hospital total revenue, reported in AHA annual survey data and Cecil G. Sheps Center analyses, is a small fraction of the site-of-service facility fee differential. [Source: AHA annual survey, Cecil G. Sheps Center uncompensated care analyses]

Part II. The Game

Ch 4. The Price Isn't the Price. LONG.

Proves: Medicare is twelve parallel payment systems, and the one that applies is selected by classification, not by the care delivered.

Sourcing. Primary in-text source for each system is the MedPAC Payment Basics one-pager series. Regulatory anchor (42 CFR and SSA provisions) in the source brackets for verification. Annual Federal Register rule citations held for endnotes, not in the body.

  1. The Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (MPFS) pays physicians and certain non-physician clinicians on a per-service basis, calculated as the sum of work, practice expense, and malpractice Relative Value Units multiplied by an annually set conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Physician and Other Health Professional Services; 42 CFR 414 subpart B]
  2. The Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) pays hospital outpatient departments on a per-encounter basis using Ambulatory Payment Classifications, each with a scaled relative weight multiplied by the OPPS conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Outpatient Hospital Services; 42 CFR 419]
  3. The Inpatient Prospective Payment System (IPPS) pays acute-care hospitals a single bundled payment per discharge based on Medicare Severity Diagnosis-Related Groups, with adjustments for wage index, disproportionate share, and graduate medical education. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Hospital Acute Inpatient Services; 42 CFR 412]
  4. The Ambulatory Surgical Center Payment System pays Medicare-certified ambulatory surgical centers on a per-procedure basis using rates set as a fraction of the OPPS APC for the same procedure, updated annually by the ASC conversion factor. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Ambulatory Surgical Center Services; 42 CFR 416]
  5. The Skilled Nursing Facility Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified skilled nursing facilities a per-diem rate adjusted for patient case mix under the Patient Driven Payment Model, covering nursing, therapy, and non-therapy ancillary components. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Skilled Nursing Facility Services; 42 CFR 413 subpart J]
  6. The Home Health Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified home health agencies a case-mix adjusted payment per 30-day period of care under the Patient-Driven Groupings Model. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Home Health Care Services; 42 CFR 484 subpart E]
  7. The Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility Prospective Payment System pays inpatient rehabilitation facilities and distinct-part rehabilitation units a per-discharge payment based on Case Mix Groups, with the facility required to meet the 60 percent rule on qualifying patient conditions to retain IRF classification. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart P]
  8. The Long-Term Care Hospital Prospective Payment System pays long-term care hospitals a per-discharge rate using Medicare Severity Long-Term Care DRGs, with a statutorily-defined site-neutral rate applying to cases that do not meet patient-criteria thresholds. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Long-Term Care Hospital Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart O]
  9. The Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System pays freestanding psychiatric hospitals and distinct-part psychiatric units a per-diem rate adjusted for patient characteristics, length of stay, and facility features. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Psychiatric Hospital Services; 42 CFR 412 subpart N]
  10. The End-Stage Renal Disease Prospective Payment System pays Medicare-certified dialysis facilities a single bundled per-treatment rate covering dialysis services, most injectable drugs, laboratory services, and supplies. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Outpatient Dialysis Services; 42 CFR 413 subpart H]
  11. Medicare pays hospice care to Medicare-certified hospices on a per-diem basis at four distinct levels of care (routine home care, continuous home care, inpatient respite care, and general inpatient care), subject to an aggregate annual cap per beneficiary. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Hospice Services; 42 CFR 418 subpart G]
  12. Critical Access Hospitals are paid 101 percent of reasonable cost for Medicare inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, and laboratory services, bypassing the prospective payment systems entirely, under designation requirements that include rural location and a 25-bed limit. [Source: MedPAC Payment Basics, Critical Access Hospital Services; 42 CFR 413.70 and 42 CFR 485 subpart F]
  13. Each of the twelve payment systems enumerated above is governed by its own statutory authority, its own annual rulemaking cycle, its own conversion factor or update mechanism, and its own set of eligibility rules for which entities can bill under it. [Source: synthesis of the twelve statutory and regulatory citations above]
  14. The payment system that applies to a given Medicare service is selected by the CMS enrollment classification of the billing entity at the time of service, not by the clinical content of the service performed. [Source: 42 CFR 424 subpart P, CMS enrollment and billing entity classification rules]

Ch 5. The Boxes. MEDIUM.

Proves: following one service from clinical order through payment reveals that the classification is a mechanical output of site and ownership, not of clinical judgment.

Anchor service. Intravenous pembrolizumab (Keytruda, HCPCS J9271) infusion, CPT 96365, administered to a Medicare beneficiary. Classification chain followed end to end: acquisition of the physician practice, CMS-855A enrollment of the site under the hospital's institutional identity, 42 CFR 413.65 compliance, POS 22 eligibility, payment split.

  1. A physician orders an intravenous pembrolizumab (Keytruda, HCPCS J9271) infusion at the standard 200 mg fixed dose every three weeks for a Medicare beneficiary with a pembrolizumab-indicated malignancy. [Source: FDA-approved pembrolizumab prescribing information; Dutch Rojas operator record for case construction]
  2. The clinical content of the infusion (drug identity, dose, administration duration, exam room, nursing staff, physician, patient) is identical whether the site bills under CMS Place of Service code 11 (office) or CMS Place of Service code 22 (on-campus hospital outpatient department). [Source: CMS Place of Service Code Set; Dutch Rojas operator record, clinical equivalence assertion]
  3. When a hospital system acquires an independent oncology practice, the physical operations of the infusion site (exam rooms, chairs, pumps, nursing staff, physicians, patient panel) remain in place while the billing entity is reassigned from the practice's CMS-855B group practice enrollment to the acquiring hospital's CMS-855A institutional enrollment. [Source: 42 CFR 424 subpart P, CMS enrollment rules for billing entity classification]
  4. The CMS-855A enrollment of the acquired site is submitted to the Medicare Administrative Contractor under the hospital's existing institutional identity and does not require any change to clinical staffing, protocols, or patient panel at the site. [Source: 42 CFR 424 subpart P; CMS Medicare Enrollment Application CMS-855A instructions]
  5. To bill services performed at the site as hospital-based under the Outpatient Prospective Payment System, the hospital documents the four criteria of 42 CFR 413.65(d): common ownership, common supervision, integrated clinical operations, and integrated financial records with the main hospital. [Source: 42 CFR 413.65(d)(1) through (d)(4)]
  6. Once the CMS-855A enrollment reflects the acquired site and the 42 CFR 413.65 compliance file is on record, the hospital can submit claims for services performed at the site using Place of Service code 22 (on-campus outpatient hospital) rather than Place of Service code 11 (office). [Source: CMS Place of Service Code Set; 42 CFR 413.65; 42 CFR 424 subpart P]
  7. Under CY2026 Medicare rates, the service-level facility fee for CPT 96365 (intravenous infusion, for therapy, prophylaxis, or diagnosis; initial, up to 1 hour) is $216.49 when billed at POS 22 under OPPS Ambulatory Payment Classification 5693 (Level 3 Drug Administration) and approximately $66.13 when billed at POS 11 under the MPFS, with the POS 11 rate derived from a CY2021 non-facility total RVU of 1.98 multiplied by the CY2026 non-QP conversion factor of $33.4009 pending operator confirmation from the CY2026 MPFS national payment amount file. [Source: CMS CY2026 January OPPS Addendum B, APC 5693 on-campus rate at the CY2026 OPPS conversion factor of $91.415; CY2021 MPFS RVU for CPT 96365 carried forward as a derived estimate, the CY2026 operator-confirmed figure from the CY2026 MPFS PFREV26B national payment amount file pending for the final swap. CY2026 policy note: excepted off-campus hospital outpatient departments billing the same code at POS 19 are paid at a PFS-equivalent rate of approximately $86.60 under the CY2026 drug administration site-neutral policy, affecting 61 HCPCS codes in APCs 5691 through 5694 at an estimated $290 million CY2026 impact; the POS 22 on-campus path remains at full OPPS and is unaffected by that policy]
  8. Pembrolizumab (Keytruda, HCPCS J9271) was the single largest Medicare Part B drug by total spending in 2023 at $4.8 billion in allowed claims, making it the highest-volume drug in the Medicare Part B program and therefore the single most consequential instance of the 340B spread mechanism traced in this chapter. [Source: MedPAC 2025 Data Book, Part B drug spending by HCPCS code]
  9. Under the April 2026 Medicare Part B Average Sales Price pricing file, a 200 mg pembrolizumab (HCPCS J9271) dose is paid at $12,250.20 under the ASP+6% methodology ($61.251 per 1 mg payment limit multiplied by 200 mg) at both POS 11 and POS 22, while a 340B covered entity acquires the same 200 mg dose at an estimated $7,674 to $8,957 using published federal average-discount anchors (OIG 33.6 percent and CMS 22.5 percent below implied ASP respectively), yielding a per-infusion drug spread of approximately $3,294 to $4,576 retained by the 340B hospital outpatient site. [Source: CMS Medicare Part B ASP Pricing File, April 2026 quarterly update, HCPCS J9271 payment limit per 1 mg; CY2018 OPPS Final Rule in which CMS set 340B Part B drug reimbursement at ASP minus 22.5 percent based on its stated estimate of the average 340B acquisition cost; HHS Office of Inspector General 2015 analysis of 340B ceiling prices estimating the average 340B discount at 33.6 percent below ASP. Note: both published discount anchors are population averages across all 340B drugs and are not pembrolizumab-specific; the Dutch Rojas operator record for the pembrolizumab-specific 340B acquisition cost is pending for the final swap and will narrow the range when it arrives]
  10. The only variable that moved between a POS 11 claim and a POS 22 claim for the same patient, same drug, same dose, and same physician is a two-digit field on the CMS-1500 or UB-04 claim form; the payment outcome was fixed upstream at the CMS-855A enrollment and 42 CFR 413.65 compliance stages, before any specific patient was scheduled for infusion. [Source: synthesis of claims 3 through 9 above; CMS-1500 and UB-04 claim form field specifications]

Ch 6. The Arbitrage. SHORT.

Proves: the only change required to triple revenue on the same service is the ownership of the billing entity.

  1. In CY2026, an independent physician oncology practice administering a 200 mg pembrolizumab infusion (HCPCS J9271, CPT 96365) to a Medicare beneficiary at Place of Service code 11 collects approximately $66.13 in MPFS service fees plus $12,250.20 in ASP+6% drug payment, yielding a per-infusion net margin of approximately $759 assuming drug acquisition at the Average Sales Price of approximately $11,557 per 200 mg dose under standard non-340B Part B buy-and-bill. Ch 7. The Trade Becomes Strategy. MEDIUM.

Proves: acquiring physician practices is not a clinical strategy, it is a billing reclassification strategy deployed at scale.

Primary source. Avalere September 2024 analysis of 100 percent Medicare Fee-for-Service claims, covering cardiology, gastroenterology, medical oncology, orthopedics, and urology. Cross-sectional risk-adjusted comparison plus pre-post difference-in-difference on physicians transitioning employment type. PAI/Avalere 2024 physician employment report for the macro employment-shift data.

  1. Approximately 77.6 percent of all U.S. physicians were employed (not in solo or small independent practice) as of January 2024, with hospital and health-system-employed physicians growing from approximately 25.8 percent of the workforce in 2012 to approximately 55.1 percent in 2024. [Source: PAI/Avalere 2024 Updated Report on Physician Employment Trends and Acquisitions of Medical Practices, 2019-2023]
  2. Across the five high-acquisition specialties of cardiology, gastroenterology, medical oncology, orthopedics, and urology, only 12 percent of physicians remained in unaffiliated private practice as of 2022, with 45 percent hospital-affiliated, 37 percent corporate-affiliated, and 6 percent private-equity-affiliated. [Source: Avalere September 2024 analysis of 100 percent Medicare FFS claims, five-specialty cross-section]
  3. Medical oncology specifically showed 5 percent unaffiliated private practice, 45 percent hospital-affiliated, and 37 percent corporate-affiliated as of 2022, meaning the specialty most directly aligned with the Ch 5 and Ch 6 Keytruda case study has lost approximately 95 percent of its independent footprint. [Source: Avalere September 2024 analysis, medical oncology subset]
  4. In risk-adjusted cross-sectional comparison of Medicare Fee-for-Service beneficiaries, those attributed to hospital-affiliated physicians generated approximately $30,416 in weighted-average expenditures per beneficiary per year, versus approximately $26,996 per beneficiary per year for beneficiaries attributed to unaffiliated private practice physicians, a gap of approximately $3,420 per beneficiary per year with no change in patient population. [Source: Avalere September 2024 analysis, cross-sectional PBPY comparison]
  5. In pre-post difference-in-difference analysis of physicians who changed employment type, the same physician transitioning from unaffiliated private practice to hospital employment was associated with a $1,327 per-beneficiary-per-year increase in Medicare expenditures (p<0.001), compared with a $1,140 per-beneficiary-per-year increase on transition to corporate employment (p<0.001) and a $963 per-beneficiary-per-year decrease on transition to private-equity-affiliated practice (p<0.001). [Source: Avalere September 2024 analysis, pre-post difference-in-difference model]
  6. Hospital-affiliated physicians directed approximately 67 percent of their total Medicare expenditures through facility-setting billing, versus approximately 60 percent for unaffiliated private practice physicians, consistent with the site-of-service classification mechanism documented in Ch 5 claims 3 through 6 and the per-infusion arbitrage documented in Ch 6 claims 1 through 4. [Source: Avalere September 2024 analysis, facility-setting expenditure share by affiliation]
  7. UnitedHealth Group's Optum subsidiary acquired DaVita Medical Group in June 2019 for approximately $4.3 billion, adding approximately 300 medical clinics and roughly 1.7 million annual patient relationships to a single vertically integrated health insurance and care-delivery platform, under a Federal Trade Commission clearance that required only a Las Vegas market divestiture. [Source: UnitedHealth Group 2019 press release; Federal Trade Commission approval order, June 2019]
  8. Moody's Investors Service 2020 and 2021 not-for-profit hospital sector outlook reports identified physician practice acquisition and outpatient revenue capture as credit-positive drivers for large hospital systems, citing economies of scale, negotiating leverage with commercial insurers, and the accelerating shift of service volume into outpatient channels. [Source: Moody's Investors Service U.S. Not-for-Profit and Public Healthcare Sector Outlooks, 2020 and 2021 editions]
  9. The 340B Drug Pricing Program, which is the mechanism behind the per-infusion drug spread documented in Ch 5 claim 9 and Ch 6 claim 2, reached approximately $81.4 billion in discounted outpatient drug purchases in calendar year 2024, an increase of approximately 23 percent over calendar year 2023, with hospital-affiliated covered entities accounting for approximately 87 percent (roughly $71 billion) of the program's total annual volume. [Source: HRSA 340B Covered Entity Purchases data for CY2023 and CY2024; Drug Channels Institute analysis of HRSA purchase data, December 2025; pre-plant for Ch 9 $275B stack, 340B stream line item of $81.4 billion]

Ch 8. The First Layer Isn't the Whole Story. SHORT.

Proves: the site-of-service gap is only the visible surface of a much larger architecture.

  1. The site-of-service payment differential, which is the per-encounter facility fee gap documented across Ch 1 through Ch 6, accounts for approximately $14.1 billion per year in federal Medicare revenue that flows to hospital outpatient departments and is unavailable to independent physician practices billing the same clinical service. [Source: The Systematic Advantage: From $125B to $275B, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, site-neutral payment violations line item]
  2. The $14.1 billion in site-of-service differential is one of eight federal funding streams flowing exclusively to hospital systems and hospital-affiliated entities under current federal payment, program, and tax law. [Source: The Systematic Advantage: From $125B to $275B, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, eight-stream enumeration]
  3. The other seven streams are Medicaid supplemental payments, the 340B Drug Pricing Program, the nonprofit hospital tax exemption, Medicare Advantage overpayments flowing to hospital-owned plans, Graduate Medical Education payments, Rural Health Transformation funding, and HRSA and federal grant programs, each governed by its own federal statutory authority and its own eligibility rule set that independent physician practices cannot satisfy. [Source: The Systematic Advantage: From $125B to $275B, Dutch Rojas, January 2026, program enumeration; program eligibility statutes at 42 USC 1396r-4 (Medicaid DSH), 42 USC 256b (340B), 26 USC 501(c)(3) (nonprofit tax exemption), 42 USC 1395w-23 (Medicare Advantage), 42 USC 1395ww(h) (GME), SUPPORT Act 2018 (Rural Health Transformation), and 42 USC 300 et seq.
