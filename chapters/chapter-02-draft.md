# Chapter 2. The First Explanation That Isn't True

The 8.3 to 1 gap in Chapter 1 has standard explanations.

They are delivered in the same three forms in every public debate over site-of-service payment. In regulatory filings. In committee testimony. In trade association statements. In the op-ed pages.

The arguments are these.

Hospitals treat sicker patients. Hospitals carry the EMTALA obligation. Hospitals absorb uncompensated care the other sites do not.

Each of the three is offered, with variations, to account for why the same operation at a hospital outpatient department costs several multiples of what it costs at an ambulatory surgical center.

Each is testable.

---

Start with acuity.

The acuity argument says the patient at the hospital outpatient department is sicker, older, or more medically complex than the patient at the ambulatory surgical center, and the facility fee reflects that added complexity. Sicker patients need more resources. More resources cost more money. More money is what the differential pays for.

Chapter 1 already disposes of the argument for the Charlotte cholecystectomy.

The patient was the same patient. The surgeon was the same surgeon. The forty-five-minute operative window was the same window. The case was not routed by acuity. It was routed by network, by referral pattern, by which operating room was open that week. Whatever the acuity of the patient, the same acuity produced a $4,200 bill at the ambulatory surgical center and a $34,700 bill at the hospital outpatient department.

The case-level evidence is one case. In isolation, it could be argued as unusual.

The systematic evidence is not in isolation.

MedPAC, the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission, is the independent federal body that advises Congress on Medicare payment policy. MedPAC has published acuity-adjusted analyses of site-of-service cost differences between hospital outpatient departments and ambulatory surgical centers. The analyses adjust for patient case mix.

After the adjustment, the payment gap persists.

The Congressional Budget Office has scored site-neutral payment proposals that would equalize reimbursement across sites for equivalent services. Its scoring projects tens of billions of dollars in Medicare savings from elimination of the differential. The scoring does not project access losses. The scoring does not project acuity losses. The savings arrive by closing the gap, not by moving sicker patients somewhere else.

Two federal analytical bodies, working independently, with different methodologies, converge on the same finding.

Adjusting for acuity does not close the gap.

The acuity argument, at the system level, does not hold.

---

Move to EMTALA.

The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act, codified at 42 USC 1395dd, is often cited as a unique burden on hospitals that ambulatory surgical centers do not carry. Hospitals have emergency departments. They are required to screen and stabilize anyone who presents, regardless of ability to pay. The facility fee, the argument goes, reflects that standing obligation.

EMTALA imposes screening and stabilization obligations. The obligation applies to hospital emergency departments.

Both propositions are true.

The site-of-service facility fee gap is not about emergency departments.

The gap arises on scheduled outpatient surgical services. Cholecystectomies. Cataracts. Colonoscopies. Arthroscopies. Services the patient books in advance. Services that arrive at a scheduled operating room at a scheduled time, with a scheduled surgeon, under a scheduled anesthetic.

EMTALA does not apply to those services. The statute's obligations do not extend to the scheduled outpatient surgical schedule. The schedule is not the emergency department. The patient is not a walk-in. There is no obligation under EMTALA to stabilize a gallbladder the patient made an appointment for.

EMTALA is a real burden. It is also a real burden on the emergency department. It is not, by the text of the statute, a burden on the outpatient surgical schedule that generates the differential.

The EMTALA argument applies to a different room in the same building.

It does not apply to the room the surgery happens in.

---

Move to uncompensated care.

Uncompensated care is the portion of hospital services delivered to patients who cannot or do not pay. Hospitals report it to the American Hospital Association annual survey. The Cecil G. Sheps Center for Health Services Research, at the University of North Carolina, analyzes the reported figures.

As a share of total hospital revenue, uncompensated care is a small fraction.

The site-of-service facility fee differential is not a small fraction of hospital revenue. It is a multiple. A four-fold or eight-fold gap on thousands of procedures per year per system, delivered as facility fees on scheduled outpatient services.

The uncompensated care line and the site-of-service differential sit on different orders of magnitude.

Charging eight times the ambulatory surgical center rate to offset a single-digit percentage of revenue is not arithmetic. It is a post-hoc justification that does not balance on its own numbers.

If uncompensated care were the reason for the differential, closing the differential would leave a single-digit percentage of revenue to be made up somewhere else. That is a recoverable number. It is not the $30,500 per gallbladder Chapter 1 described.

---

The three explanations do not survive contact with the data they are offered against.

Acuity does not survive the MedPAC case-mix adjustment or the CBO scoring.

EMTALA does not survive the text of the statute.

Uncompensated care does not survive the arithmetic of uncompensated care as a share of revenue against the site-of-service multiples.

The patient in Charlotte is still waiting.

The invoice still arrives at a single facility line. The patient's share still reflects the building's rate. The $30,500 gap between the lowest and highest facility payments for the same forty-five-minute operation still sits somewhere the patient cannot see.

If the gap is not a function of acuity, and not a function of EMTALA, and not a function of uncompensated care, the gap is a function of something else.

That is the question Chapter 3 begins to answer.
