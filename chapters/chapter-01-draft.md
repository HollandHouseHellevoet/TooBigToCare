# Chapter 1. The Same Procedure

A patient in Charlotte, North Carolina is scheduled for a gallbladder removal.

The scheduling is routine. A referral from a primary physician. A consultation with a surgeon. A decision that the operation is indicated. Consent forms, pre-operative instructions, a time and a place to arrive. A confirmation call the day before. An appointment on the books.

The clinical term is laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The billing code is CPT 47562. In cases that include an additional intraoperative step, the code is CPT 47563. Four small ports. A camera. A forty-five-minute operative window. The gallbladder is removed intact.

The patient does not select the facility.

The facility is determined by referral pattern. By insurance network. By proximity. By which operating room has an opening on the needed date. The patient is told where to arrive. The patient arrives.

Three facilities in the Charlotte market can perform this procedure.

The first is an ambulatory surgical center. Accredited. Staffed. Equipped with the same laparoscopic tower a hospital has. Operating rooms dedicated to scheduled outpatient surgery. Patients in the morning. Patients out in the afternoon. The case mix is scheduled surgery. The staffing is scheduled. The supply chain is scheduled.

The second is a physician-owned surgical facility. Accredited. Staffed. Equipped. The ownership structure differs from the ambulatory surgical center and differs from the hospital outpatient department. The building is a building. The operating room is an operating room. The laparoscopic tower is a laparoscopic tower. The surgical team is the same kind of surgical team.

The third is a hospital outpatient department operated by Atrium Health. Accredited. Staffed. Equipped. It is part of a larger hospital system. Its operating rooms are operating rooms. Its recovery bays are recovery bays. Its laparoscopic towers are laparoscopic towers. Its schedule is the same kind of schedule.

From the hallway, from the operating table, from the recovery bay, and from the patient's side of the experience, the three buildings do the same thing for this patient. They accept the referral. They place the patient in a preoperative bay. They transfer the patient to an operating room. They perform the procedure. They move the patient to recovery. They discharge the patient the same day.

---

The physician who performs the operation is the same across all three.

The patient is the same.

The operative window is the same forty-five minutes.

The technique is the same. The ports are placed in the same anatomical positions. The same small camera is inserted. The gallbladder is extracted through the same umbilical incision. The closure is the same closure. The post-operative instructions are the same post-operative instructions.

The clinical content of the operation is identical across the three sites.

The payment is not.

---

At the ambulatory surgical center, Medicare's locality-adjusted payment for the procedure is approximately $4,200.

The figure is observed directly in operator records from the Charlotte market. It is what the ambulatory surgical center receives for performing the procedure on a Medicare beneficiary after the Charlotte locality adjustment is applied.

The CY2026 national unadjusted ASC rate for CPT 47562, published by CMS in Addendum AA, is $3,030.97. The national figure is the starting point. The locality adjustment to Charlotte lifts the figure into the $4,200 range. Both numbers describe the same procedure. One is a published federal schedule. The other is what the schedule becomes when Charlotte-specific locality factors are applied.

That is the first number.

---

At the physician-owned surgical facility in the same market, the same procedure is paid approximately $6,800.

The figure is observed in operator records from the Charlotte market. Same physician. Same operation. Same forty-five minutes. Different building, different number. The figure is not locality-adjusted from some other number. It is the rate the facility receives on this procedure in this market.

That is the second number.

---

At Atrium Health's hospital outpatient department, the negotiated commercial rate for the same procedure is approximately $34,700.

This is a commercial rate, not a Medicare rate. It reflects what a commercial payer pays Atrium Health's hospital outpatient department under a negotiated contract. It is observed in operator records from the Charlotte market. The contract is the contract. The rate is the rate.

That is the third number.

The first number is a Medicare figure. The second is an operator-observed facility rate. The third is a commercial contracted rate. Three payment mechanisms, three buildings, three numbers, one forty-five-minute operation.

---

$4,200.

$6,800.

$34,700.

Same patient. Same physician. Same forty-five minutes. Same four ports. Same camera. Same gallbladder. Same umbilical closure. Same discharge instructions.

Three buildings. Three numbers.

---

The ratio between the highest and the lowest is approximately 8.3 to 1.

Eight point three to one is not a rounding artifact. It is not a statistical outlier. It is not a function of one unusual patient. It is not a function of one unusual contract. It is not a function of a single negotiation that went unusually one way.

It is the observed rate for moving the same operation, performed by the same physician in the same forty-five minutes, across the Charlotte market.

$4,200 at one address. $34,700 at another address in the same market. A difference of $30,500 on a single outpatient surgery. A ratio of approximately 8.3 to 1.

The ratio applies to one procedure on one day. If the surgeon performs five of these procedures in a week, and all five are routed to the hospital outpatient department instead of the ambulatory surgical center, the difference on that week's schedule is approximately $152,500. If a hundred of these procedures are routed to the hospital outpatient department instead of the ambulatory surgical center over a year, the difference is approximately $3,050,000. The arithmetic scales cleanly. The ratio is linear.

The variable the patient controls is the operation.

The variable someone else controls is the building.

---

The patient receives a bill downstream.

The bill is generated from the contracted rate. The contracted rate is run through the patient's plan design. The plan design applies a deductible, a copayment, a coinsurance percentage, an out-of-pocket maximum. The result is a share of the total, returned to the patient as the patient's balance.

The patient's share is calibrated against the contracted rate, not against the clinical content. The more expensive the building, the more expensive the share. A deductible applied to $4,200 applies more lightly than a deductible applied to $34,700. A coinsurance percentage taken against the higher contracted rate pulls the patient's share up in direct proportion. An out-of-pocket maximum is reached faster against the higher contracted rate than against the lower.

The patient does not see the three numbers side by side.

The patient sees one number. The one attached to the building they were routed to.

The $4,200 version of the procedure and the $34,700 version of the procedure are not presented to the patient as a choice.

They are not presented at all.

The invoice arrives in the mail. The invoice arrives as a statement from the insurer. The invoice arrives as a balance due from the facility. The invoice arrives with a single facility line. The line shows the charged amount, an adjustment, the contracted amount, the plan's allowed payment, and the patient's balance due. The invoice is the only view of the transaction the patient is given.

The $30,500 gap between the lowest and highest facility payments for the same forty-five-minute operation is not on the invoice. The invoice does not reference the other two buildings. The invoice does not reference the classification of the building the procedure happened in. The invoice references a single line, a single balance, a single address.

The three-building comparison is not anywhere the patient can see it.

---

The surgeon did not set the numbers.

The surgeon performed the procedure. The surgeon billed for the professional component of the operation, which is paid under a separate schedule from the facility fee. The professional fee compensates the surgeon for the work of performing the surgery. The facility fee compensates the building for the room, the staff, the equipment, the supplies.

The surgeon's professional fee does not vary by a factor of 8.3 across the three buildings.

The facility fee does.

The building sets the numbers.

More precisely: the classification attached to the building sets the numbers.

Ambulatory surgical center.

Physician-owned surgical facility.

Hospital outpatient department.

Three classifications. Three fee schedules. Three rates for the same clinical act.

The classification was not chosen on the day of surgery. The classification was established upstream, by statute, by regulation, by enrollment paperwork filed months or years before the patient was scheduled. The patient cannot see the classification. The surgeon cannot change the classification. The operating room staff cannot change the classification.

By the time the patient arrives at the building, the classification has already decided what Medicare and the commercial payer will pay.

By the time the patient arrives at the chair, the number is already set.

---

There is no clinical justification inside these three numbers.

There is no case-mix explanation inside these three numbers. There is no acuity gradient. There is no additional service rendered at the $34,700 site that is absent at the $4,200 site.

The four ports go in the same places. The camera is the same camera. The gallbladder is the same gallbladder. The surgeon is the same surgeon. The patient is the same patient. The forty-five minutes is the same forty-five minutes.

The $30,500 difference is not a function of the operation.

It is a function of the classification of the room the operation happens in.

---

One procedure. One market. One surgeon. One patient.

$4,200 at the ambulatory surgical center.

$6,800 at the physician-owned surgical facility.

$34,700 at Atrium Health's hospital outpatient department.

Eight point three to one.

The operation is unchanged across the three sites. The forty-five minutes is unchanged. The ports, the camera, the closure, the discharge: unchanged.

What moves is the classification of the room.

The classification sits upstream of the patient. Upstream of the surgeon. Upstream of the procedure itself. The classification is the input. The rate is the output. The rate is not the output of the operation. The operation is identical across the three rates. The rate is the output of the classification, and the classification is decided before the surgery is scheduled.

A gallbladder removal in Charlotte, North Carolina costs $4,200 at one address and $34,700 at another address, with the same surgeon performing the same operation on the same patient in the same forty-five minutes. The three addresses sit inside three classifications. The classifications sit inside a larger architecture. The architecture sits inside the statute and the regulation that produced it.

The patient's invoice shows none of that.

The patient's invoice shows one line. One balance. One address.

The classification is the mechanism this book is about.
